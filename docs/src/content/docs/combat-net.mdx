Search often stops inside a combat that is not over, and it then needs to know what the current position is worth. The **combat net** reads the position and predicts how the combat will end: death, or a win, with how much more HP lost and whether the potions in hand are still there. Each ending is scored by what the player carries out of the combat: HP + max HP + f of each potion kept, where **f** is a small network that says how many HP a potion will save in a later combat. The scores are weighted by probability. The combat net says only what will happen; what that is worth is counted from it.

The figure below shows its predictions on 16 real positions. The red bar is the chance of death, the other 50 bars are the endings of a win, and the grey ones cannot happen. For example, on turn 6 against the Waterfall Giant, the act 1 boss, the Fool has 27/77 HP left and only Strikes and Defends in hand: the net is all but sure that it dies.

## What it reads

The net reads what the player can see at the start of a turn:

- **Cards**: for each card in the four piles, which card it is, whether it is upgraded, and the numbers this combat has changed on it, such as its current cost or damage that has grown.
- **The player**: HP, Block, Energy and the current turn number.
- **Status effects**: for each status effect on the player and the enemies, which kind it is and how many stacks it has.
- **Enemies**: who each one is, its HP, Block and current intent, plus the intents it showed over the previous three turns.
- **The rest**: relics, potions, which character is playing, which encounter this is, the Ascension level and, in the Dread mode, the dominion over this combat.

It does not read the draw order, the random numbers or the enemies' queued next moves. So its values cannot leak anything hidden, and the same net can be used in both environments.

It does not read the numbers printed on a card either. That Strike deals 6 damage is something the net learns by itself from "this card is Strike". No field in the input says whether a card is good or bad.

## How a pile of things becomes a string of numbers

The number of things in a combat is not fixed: the hand may hold 3 cards or 10. The net handles this in three steps:

1. **Each thing becomes 64 numbers.** A card, a status effect or an enemy is first looked up in a table by what kind it is, then adjusted by its own numbers.
2. **Things in the same pile are added up.** Each card passes through the same small network by itself, and the results for one pile are then added together. Adding ignores order and works for any number of cards. The net uses a sum and not an average because only a sum tells a full hand from a thin one.
3. **Joined end to end, then through two layers.** All the sums are joined into 1,294 numbers, which pass through a network of two layers with 512 units each. Only here do the cards, the enemies and the HP meet for the first time.

The whole network has about 1.85 million weights. The six characters share one network, and the character is only a tag in the input.

## What it outputs

**The distribution of endings.** There are 51 probabilities in all: death, plus 50 bands. Each band says what share of max HP will still be lost between now and winning this combat. The bands run from −25% to 100%, and a negative band means the combat ends with HP gained instead.

- **It predicts how much is still to be lost, not how much is left at the end.** The current HP is known exactly. When search compares two positions, the one with more HP is already ahead before the net says a word.
- **Impossible endings are switched off.** A player with 30% of HP left cannot lose another 50% and still win. The probability of those bands is exactly 0. This rule is written into the net, and training and real play use the same one.

**Potions.** For each potion in hand, the net separately predicts the chance that it has been used by the time the combat is won. Wins with heavy HP loss and wins with light HP loss are predicted apart, because a narrow win is often bought by drinking a potion.

## How it becomes one number

For each possible ending, the AI works out how much HP is left then and which potions remain, and scores it: HP + max HP + f of each potion kept. It then adds these up, weighted by the probability of each ending. Death scores 0.

The net does not output a score directly, for three reasons. No number has to be set by a person. Each ending is scored by its own HP and potions, so a narrow win and a big win are not mixed together. And the same calculation also gives two numbers a player can understand: the chance of winning this combat and the average HP left after a win.

HP is counted as it is and not judged by V, the network that values a whole run. V learned from whole runs, where the HP left after a fight also says how the run was built, so it can rate less HP higher. Counted one for one, more HP always scores more.

## How it learns

A sample is the start of each turn in self-play, and its label is how that combat really ended. The net the champion uses learned from about 69,000 runs and about 6.55 million turns. One run in five is held back from learning and used only for checking.

On the runs held back for checking, among positions from the same encounter and the same turn, the net tells death from survival with a score of about 0.94. On this score 1 is perfect, 0.5 is guessing, and looking at HP alone gives 0.71. The death rate it predicts is 6.34%, and the real one is 5.93%.

## What a potion kept is worth

**f** answers one question: how many HP will this potion save in the combat where it is drunk? It is built on V and reads what V reads: the deck, relics, potions, the map ahead, gold, the floor and the rest. HP and max HP it reads only as their average. So a potion is worth the same at any HP, and an ending's score can never rise as its HP falls. It also makes f cheap: the endings that keep the same potions share one answer.

On top of V's layers it takes the potion asked about and passes both through one more layer. The answer is in HP and never below 0, so keeping a potion never scores less than throwing it away, and a potion is never drunk for nothing.

**Its labels come from the combat net.** For each potion held at a decision outside combat, the record of the run finds the combat where it was later drunk. At that combat's first turn, the combat net predicts twice what the player carries out of it: with the potion, and with the potion taken off the belt. The difference is the label. A potion that left the belt outside a combat, or was still held when the run ended, saved nothing and is labelled 0.

f starts from the champion V's layers and learned from about 125,000 runs. On the runs held back for checking, its answer is typically off by 6.64 HP, against 7.17 HP for giving each kind of potion its own average.

## What it cannot see

- **Only HP, max HP and potions are scored.** Gold stolen in the combat or a card grown in it is not part of an ending's score.
- **Cards cannot see each other.** Each card is computed by itself before the sum, so how two cards work together can be learned only by the two layers that follow.
- **It learns SL's endings.** Every run it was trained on was played in SL, so in NoSL it estimates as if a stronger player were playing.
- **Where it is wrong, f is wrong too.** f's labels are this net's own estimates.

V is a neural network that reads a run and predicts how it will end. This section describes the current one, **pure V** (`learn/v/`), as v6-3-2 has it: what it reads, how each layer transforms it, and why it is built that way. Then §4 describes the **encoders' net**, the architecture the next plan trains next to it. How its output becomes a value, and how it is trained, is the [next section](/en/arch/macro-brain/value/).

## 1. What V reads

Pure V reads **what the run is and its raw state, nothing else**. No feature written by hand, no card's damage or cost, no relic's effect: what matters in a card, a relic or a deck is the net's to learn from how runs end.

The inputs, all read from the run view that Harness logs at every decision ([Harness §6](/en/arch/harness/#sec-6)):

- **Each card of the deck:** its id, its upgrade level (at most 3) and the ids of its enchantment and affliction, if any.
- **Each relic:** its id, with `:used` once it is used up. **Each potion:** its id.
- **The context:** the character, the act's id, the act's boss or bosses and the act's ancient.
- **The map:** every point a path leads to from where the player stands (the last visited point, or the start before any), each as one token: its type plus how many floors ahead it is, such as `Elite+3`, with `:quest` where a quest marks it.
- **Six numbers:** the floor, HP, max HP, gold, the ascension and the potion slots. Each is standardized, $(x - \mu)/\sigma$, by the mean and standard deviation of the training data, which the net file keeps.

An id seen fewer than 3 times in the training data reads as one shared *unknown* id. v6-3-2's tables hold 509 card ids, 21 modifiers, 260 relic ids, 53 potions, 26 context tokens and 133 map tokens, each counting the unknown one. The one specification of all this is `learn/v/encode.py`; the brain's `RunNet` (its `Tokens`, `MapTokens` and the state) and this page's figures follow it.

The map is read as a *bag* of these tokens: V knows that an elite lies three floors ahead and a rest site four floors ahead, but not whether the rest site comes after the elite on the same path. The encoders' net reads the paths ([§4](#sec-4)).

**Why no hand-written knowledge.** The first V (V v0, `RunValue`) was 17 hand-written features of the run times weights in units of one HP: a potion was worth 8 HP, a relic 8, an upgraded card 3, a gold 0.1, each card in the deck −0.3, plus the deck's mean damage, block and draw. A later net, n2-b0.03, read those 17 features as inputs and was pulled towards V v0's choices in training. Its choices followed the prices. On the SL runs of `promotion-1` (160 for n2-b0.03, 200 for the others):

| | n2-b0.03 (read V v0's features) | pure-1-g (pure V) | random choices |
|---|---|---|---|
| rest site at 70–85% HP: share that rested | 99% | 51% | 48% |
| rest site at 85–99% HP: share that rested | 35% | 7% | 53% |
| potions bought | 417 | 330 | 92 |
| cards bought | 55 | 313 | 162 |
| gold left when leaving a shop (mean) | 89 | 27 | 36 |

A potion at 8 HP for 50–75 gold (5–7.5 HP at the price of gold) always looked like a bargain, and a heal of 21 HP always beat an upgrade worth 3. A price written by hand is a strategy written by hand, and it is wrong in ways that runs cannot correct, since the net copies it. Pure V has no prices; its only teacher is the run's outcome.

## 2. The architecture

The net turns the tokens into one vector per card, pools the deck, sums each bag, appends the numbers, and passes the result through a body of two layers to its heads. The diagram shows it from the inputs at the bottom to the output at the top; point at a block for what it computes.

<ArchDiagram client:visible lang="en" net="pure" />

In formulas, for a deck of $n$ cards:

**Card vectors.** Card $k$ has an id $c_k$, an upgrade level $u_k \le 3$ and modifier ids $m$ (its enchantment and affliction). Its input vector, 32 numbers, is

$$
x_k = E[c_k] + u_k\, U[c_k] + \sum_{m \in \mathrm{mods}(k)} M[m],
$$

where $E$, $U$ and $M$ are learned tables (*embeddings*): one vector per card id, one *upgrade direction* per card id, one vector per modifier. An upgrade moves a card along a direction the net learns for that card.

**The set encoder.** A small network $\varphi$ (32 → 128 → 128, ReLU) maps each card on its own, and the deck is pooled two ways:

$$
h_k = \varphi(x_k), \qquad \bar h = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} h_k, \qquad \tilde h = \frac{1}{16}\sum_{k=1}^{n} h_k .
$$

The mean $\bar h$ says what the deck is made of; the scaled sum $\tilde h$ also says how much of it there is (the factor $1/16$ keeps it about a mean's size).

**The bags.** Relics, potions, context and map are each the sum of their tokens' 32-number vectors: $r = \sum_i R[\text{relic}_i]$, and the same for the other three.

**The body.** The deck's two pools, the four bags and the six standardized numbers are concatenated into one vector of $128 + 128 + 4 \cdot 32 + 6 = 390$ numbers, $z$, and

$$
b_1 = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_1 z + c_1), \qquad b_2 = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_2 b_1 + c_2),
$$

each of 256 numbers. **The heads** read $b_2$: for each floor $g$ from 0 to 63, a *hazard* $h_g = \sigma(w_g^\top b_2 + a_g)$, the chance that the run ends on floor $g$ if it is still alive there; and, for the gauntlet's V, a *share* $s_g$ by a second set of weights ([From hazards to value §1](/en/arch/macro-brain/value/#sec-1)). A third head, used only in training, is not in the net file ([From hazards to value §3](/en/arch/macro-brain/value/#sec-3)).

| part | shape | weights |
|---|---|---|
| card, upgrade, modifier embeddings | 509 × 32, 509 × 32, 21 × 32 | 33,248 |
| relic, potion, context, map embeddings | 260, 53, 26, 133 × 32 | 15,104 |
| $\varphi$ | 32 → 128 → 128 | 20,736 |
| body | 390 → 256 → 256 | 165,888 |
| hazard and share heads | 256 → 64, twice | 32,896 |
| **total** | | **267,872** |

## 3. Why a set encoder

A deck is a *multiset*: the order of its cards means nothing, and its size varies from about 10 to 40. A net that read the cards in a sequence would have to learn that every order is the same; a fixed-size vector of counts per card would have hundreds of mostly empty slots and nothing shared between similar cards. The architecture above is a **Deep Set** (Zaheer et al., 2017): a function of the form $\rho\left(\sum_k \varphi(x_k)\right)$ gives the same output for every order of its inputs and reads sets of any size, and every such function can be written in that form. Here $\varphi$ is the card network and $\rho$ the body.

Each card id has its own learned vector, so the net starts knowing nothing about any card. What it learns about a card comes from the runs whose decks held it, through every decision of those runs. That is why ids seen fewer than 3 times read as unknown: a vector trained on one or two runs is noise.

The design has a known limit. Cards meet each other, and the relics, only in the body, after the deck is pooled into 128 numbers twice. A relation such as "Shivs are worth more with Shuriken and Kunai" has to pass through those pooled numbers before it can meet the relic bag. A layer of attention between the cards and relics before pooling lets them interact directly; that is the encoders' net's build encoder ([§4](#sec-4)).

## 4. The encoders' net

The next plan trains a second architecture next to pure V: the **encoders' net** (`EncoderNet` in `learn/v/net.py`, trained with `v.train --encoders`; its first net is to be v7-1e). It reads the same ids and numbers as pure V and keeps its body and heads. Only the input side changes, in the two places where pure V is weakest:

- A **build encoder** replaces the deck's set encoder and the relic and potion bags. Cards, relics and potions become one set whose elements act on each other before the set is pooled.
- A **route encoder** replaces the map's bag. It reads the reachable points as a graph of paths, not as a bag of tokens.

The context bag and the six numbers stay as they are.

### The build encoder

Every card, relic and potion of the run becomes one element of a single set, a vector of 64 numbers:

$$
x =
\begin{cases}
E[c] + u\, U[c] + \sum_{m} M[m] + v_{\text{card}} & \text{a card,}\\[2pt]
R[r] + v_{\text{relic}} & \text{a relic,}\\[2pt]
P[p] + v_{\text{potion}} & \text{a potion.}
\end{cases}
$$

For a card with id $c$, upgrade level $u$ and modifiers $m$, the first line is pure V's card vector ([§2](#sec-2)), now 64 numbers wide; $r$ and $p$ are a relic's and a potion's ids. $v_{\text{card}}$, $v_{\text{relic}}$ and $v_{\text{potion}}$ are learned *kind vectors*: they tell the net which of the three kinds an element is.

The set then goes through two **self-attention blocks** (Vaswani et al., 2017). Self-attention lets each element look at every other element of the set and take in a weighted mix of them. The element computes the weights from its own vector and theirs. One block does two updates, each added back to the element:

$$
x \leftarrow x + W_o\, \mathrm{MHA}(\mathrm{LN}(x)), \qquad x \leftarrow x + \mathrm{FF}(\mathrm{LN}(x)).
$$

- **LN** is a layer normalization: each vector is shifted and scaled to mean 0 and variance 1.
- **FF** is a small network (64 → 128 → 64, ReLU) applied to each element on its own.
- **MHA** is multi-head attention: 4 heads of 16 numbers each, run side by side and joined back into 64 numbers.

Within one head, element $i$ forms a query $q_i$, and every element $j$ a key $k_j$ and a value $v_j$, each a learned linear map of $\mathrm{LN}(x)$. Then

$$
a_{ij} = \frac{\exp\!\big(q_i^\top k_j / 4\big)}{\sum_{j'} \exp\!\big(q_i^\top k_{j'} / 4\big)}, \qquad y_i = \sum_j a_{ij}\, v_j .
$$

So $a_{ij}$ is how much element $i$ attends to element $j$, and the 4 is $\sqrt{16}$, the usual scaling.

A batch pads runs of different sizes to one length with empty slots. Each empty slot's score is lowered by $10^9$ before the softmax, so no element attends to it.

After the two blocks, a last layer normalization, and the set is pooled as pure V pools the deck: its mean and its sum times $1/16$, 128 numbers.

In pure V, a card meets the relics only in the body, after the deck is pooled into numbers that no longer say which card is which ([§3](#sec-3)). Here a Shiv's vector can change because Shuriken and Kunai are in the set, before anything is pooled. This is the idea of the Set Transformer (Lee et al., 2019), applied to the whole loadout. The output still does not depend on the elements' order, since attention and pooling treat every element alike. Like pure V, it reads ids only, with no "attack" or "skill" tag written by hand.

### The route encoder

The map's bag tells pure V that an elite lies three floors ahead and a rest site four floors ahead. It cannot tell whether the rest site comes after the elite on the same path, or on another path the player would have to choose instead. The route encoder reads the paths themselves.

**Nodes** (`encode.route`). Node 0 is the player's point. Then come the points still reachable, in order of row, then column. Each node has four fields:
- its type (monster, elite, rest site, merchant, unknown, treasure, ancient, boss);
- how many floors ahead of the player it is, at most 16;
- whether a quest marks it;
- the indices of the nodes its links lead to.

On 1,242 states of low-budget whole runs, a state had up to 69 reachable points (22 on average), and no point led to more than 5. The boss is the last point, on row 15 or 16 of the act's map.

**Start.** Each node starts as $s_i = T[\text{type}_i] + A[\text{ahead}_i] + q_i\, v_{\text{quest}}$, 32 numbers, where $q_i$ is 1 for a quest mark and 0 otherwise.

**Rounds.** Then, 16 times, every node is made anew from its start and the nodes it leads to:

$$
h_i \leftarrow \mathrm{ReLU}\Big( W \big[\, s_i \,;\; \max_{j \in \mathrm{next}(i)} h_j \,;\; \operatorname{mean}_{j \in \mathrm{next}(i)} h_j \,\big] + b \Big),
$$

- $[\,\cdot\,;\cdot\,]$ joins vectors end to end.
- The max is taken number by number.
- $h = s$ before the first round.
- The boss leads nowhere, so its max and mean are 0.
- Every round uses the same $W$ and $b$.

After $k$ rounds, a node's vector depends on the nodes up to $k$ steps ahead. Information thus moves back from the boss one floor per round. An act's map has 16 rows above its start, so 16 rounds carry the boss to any node.

This is a learned **dynamic program** back from the boss. The classic one computes the best path's value from each point as $\text{value}(i) = \text{reward}(i) + \max_{j \in \text{next}(i)} \text{value}(j)$, from the boss back. The round has the same shape:
- vectors stand in for single values;
- a learned $W$ stands in for the addition;
- the mean of the next points joins the max. A run does not always get to follow its best path, so what the other paths hold matters too.

In the terms of graph networks, it is **message passing** (Gilmer et al., 2017) along the map's links, backwards only, with one shared update.

**Output.** The player's node's vector $h_0$, and the mean over all nodes, 64 numbers in all. The first is "what lies ahead of me along the paths"; the second summarizes the rest of the act.

### The body, the size and the cost

The body reads $128 + 64 + 64 + 6 = 262$ numbers: the build, the context bag (now 64 numbers wide), the route, and the six numbers. Then it is pure V's body: 256 → 256 with ReLU, then the hazard head. With the next plan's objective `win`, the value is the chance to win, $S_{63}$ ([From hazards to value §1](/en/arch/macro-brain/value/#sec-1)).

| part | shape | weights |
|---|---|---|
| card, upgrade, modifier embeddings | 509 × 64, 509 × 64, 21 × 64 | 66,496 |
| relic, potion, context embeddings | 260, 53, 26 × 64 | 21,696 |
| kind and quest vectors | 3 × 64, 32 | 224 |
| type and floors-ahead embeddings | 9 × 32, 17 × 32 | 832 |
| two self-attention blocks | 64 wide, 4 heads, feed-forward 128 | 66,432 |
| route update $W, b$ | 96 → 32 | 3,104 |
| body | 262 → 256 → 256 | 133,120 |
| hazard head | 256 → 64 | 16,448 |
| **total** | | **308,352** |

The table counts the embeddings with v6-3-2's table sizes and the eight point types plus the unknown id. v7-1e's tables will come from its own training data. As in §2, the training-only head is left out.

Most of the extra cost is in the 16 rounds and the attention. In the brain, `RunNet` (`src/Sts2Ai.Brain/Macro/RunNet.cs`) builds the route's tensors and feeds them only when the net file's graph declares a `route` input, so every older net loads as before. A full-size encoders' net takes 0.44 ms a call in C#, against 0.093 ms for v6-3-2. A macro decision on the clone takes 70–90 ms, so the difference does not matter.

**Tests.** The ONNX check of [§2](#sec-2) applies as before: the brain refuses a net file unless it gives the test runs the trainer's values. Beyond that, a tiny untrained encoders' net (small widths, weights scaled up, `src/Sts2Ai.Tests/Fixtures/pure-v-tiny-encoders.json`) is run on three real views. Its values move by 10 times the load check's tolerance when the route loses its links or its floors ahead, and 3 times when every type reads as unknown. So a route encoder that silently ignored the map would fail the tests.

Two details come from the export, not from the design. The layer normalizations have no scale and shift of their own; the linear layer after each carries them. The kind and quest vectors start random, not at zero. Both are needed because the ONNX export merges weights of equal value, which would drop their names from the file.
